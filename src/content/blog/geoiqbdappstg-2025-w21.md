# Enhanced PPT Generation: Report & Market Automation for LK and Bata

## Overview

The 21st week of 2025 saw significant advancements in our geospatial intelligence platform, primarily focusing on streamlining and enhancing the Power Point Presentation (PPT) generation workflow within the `geoiq_bd_app_stg` repository. This weekly engineering cycle, marked by a robust 58 commits, was dedicated to addressing critical bottlenecks and expanding our client-specific reporting capabilities.

Our core efforts revolved around two major initiatives: first, automating the creation of essential property reports that were previously a manual prerequisite for PPT generation; and second, developing a sophisticated, dynamic market creation system tailored specifically for Lenskart (LK) properties. This latter initiative involved complex geospatial analysis, intricate integrations with external data and mapping APIs, and even the application of AI for intelligent market naming. Concurrently, we laid the groundwork for integrating Bata's specific PPT generation requirements and undertook a series of general debugging and utility improvements to bolster the application's overall stability and performance. The overarching goal was to reduce manual intervention, improve data completeness, and accelerate the delivery of high-quality, data-rich presentations to our key clients.

## Problem

Before this week's concentrated efforts, several critical issues hampered the efficiency and reliability of our PPT generation process, particularly concerning data availability and market definition.

Firstly, a significant point of failure in the PPT generation flow occurred when a property lacked an existing report ID (`ap_tr_id`). This meant that if a property had not undergone a prior analysis and report generation, the entire PPT creation process would halt, requiring manual intervention from an analyst to pre-generate the report. This manual step introduced delays, increased operational overhead, and was a frequent source of frustration, especially when dealing with new or recently surveyed properties. The system was not self-sufficient in handling these common scenarios, leading to fragmented workflows and inconsistent turnaround times for client deliverables.

Secondly, a recurring challenge with surveyed Lenskart (LK) properties was their tendency to occasionally fall outside the boundaries of our predefined market polygons. Our existing market definitions, while comprehensive, could not account for every potential new store location, particularly in rapidly expanding or less-dense areas. When an LK property's latitude and longitude coordinates did not intersect with an established market, it resulted in incomplete data for the property. This absence of market context severely limited the depth of analysis possible for these properties, impacting the accuracy and richness of the generated PPTs, which rely heavily on market-level demographic and competitive insights. A dynamic mechanism was clearly needed to ensure every LK property was associated with a relevant market, whether existing or newly defined.

Thirdly, the implementation of these new features necessitated extensive integration with a suite of external geospatial and data APIs, as well as an advanced Large Language Model (LLM). Specifically, we needed to interact with the Valhalla isochrone service for calculating drive-time polygons, the `getvariables-internal-data-in` endpoint for retrieving demographic and Point-of-Interest (POI) variables, and OpenAI's GPT-4o-mini for intelligent market naming. Each of these integrations presented its own set of challenges, from correctly formatting request payloads and handling diverse response structures to managing authentication and rate limits. Debugging these interactions, especially regarding precise data types (e.g., `json.dumps(data)` vs `json=data` for `requests.post`), consumed considerable engineering time and required meticulous attention to detail to ensure robust and reliable data exchange.

Fourth, ensuring robust and consistent database connections for GeoPandas operations across different database types proved challenging. Our application interacts with multiple PostgreSQL instances, including `geoiqdb`, `geoiqdataserving`, and `geoiq_retail_app_v3`, each potentially having slightly different connection parameters or data schemas. GeoPandas, being a powerful tool for spatial data manipulation, relies on stable and correctly configured database connections to perform spatial queries efficiently. Managing these connections and ensuring compatibility across various database environments added a layer of complexity to the geospatial processing tasks.

Finally, during the development and testing phases of these new PPT generation features, there was a significant risk of accidentally triggering client notifications (e.g., email or WhatsApp messages) that are typically sent upon successful report generation. Such unintended communications could lead to confusion, provide premature or incomplete information, and potentially damage client trust. A robust mechanism was required to prevent these notifications from firing during development, ensuring a safe and controlled testing environment without disrupting client communications.

## Solution

To address the identified problems, a multi-faceted engineering approach was adopted, focusing on automation, dynamic data generation, and robust integration.

The first critical problem, the failure of PPT generation due to missing report IDs, was resolved by implementing a new `generate_report_for_property` method. This method was designed to proactively check if a property's `ap_tr_id` (Application Property Transaction ID) was present. If missing, it would automatically trigger a call to an internal API endpoint, `retailapp/stg/v3/store/generateReport`, to create the necessary report on demand. This automation, introduced in commits like `7c3b87f18a70` and refined in `d2c08d78cb5b` (which fixed the endpoint to the correct staging URL), eliminated the manual intervention bottleneck, ensuring a seamless and uninterrupted PPT generation workflow. The `2fff1ebc97ad` commit specifically addressed debugging the `requests.post` payload, transitioning from `data=data` to `data=json.dumps(data)` to satisfy the API's strict JSON body requirement, highlighting the meticulous detail required for robust API integration.

The challenge of Lenskart (LK) properties falling outside predefined market boundaries was tackled with the development of a comprehensive `create_custom_market_for_LK` function. This sophisticated function, initially introduced in commit `a2ca4a8f9e3a`, orchestrates a series of steps:
1.  **Market Check:** It first verifies if a given LK property's latitude and longitude coordinates already fall within an existing market using spatial queries. This is facilitated by the refined `DBUtil.get_gpd_geodataframe` method, which ensures compatibility with GeoPandas across `geoiqdb`, `geoiqdataserving`, and `geoiq_retail_app_v3` databases for efficient spatial lookups.
2.  **Isochrone Generation:** If no existing market is found, the system leverages Google Cloud Functions to call the Valhalla isochrone service. This service calculates a drive-time polygon (e.g., a 15-minute drive-time area) around the property, effectively defining its immediate trade area. Commit `a2ca4a8f9e3a` specifically shows the integration of `get_iscohrone` for this purpose.
3.  **Demographic and POI Data Retrieval:** Concurrently, another Google Cloud Function is invoked to query the `getvariables-internal-data-in` API. This retrieves crucial demographic data and Points of Interest (POIs) within the newly defined isochrone polygon, enriching the market's profile.
4.  **AI-Driven Market Naming:** To provide intuitive and permanent names for these dynamically generated markets, we integrated OpenAI's GPT-4o-mini. The `get_market_name_from_lat_lng` function, part of the `a2ca4a8f9e3a` commit, feeds nearby POI addresses and location context to the LLM, which then intelligently derives a human-readable market name. This ensures consistency and avoids generic naming conventions.
5.  **Market Persistence:** Finally, all the generated market attributes, including the geospatial polygon, demographic data, POIs, and the AI-derived name, are persisted in our database, making the custom market available for future analyses and PPT generations. The extensive commit cluster related to LK market creation (e.g., `a2ca4a8f9e3a` through `a3a009f65e7e`) reflects the iterative debugging and refinement from "v0 to v23" to perfect this complex process.

To prepare for future client integrations, placeholder methods were added for new client-specific survey templates, such as for Bata and Purplle. Commit `33660bb38d40` explicitly introduces `survey_template__bata`, laying the groundwork for integrating their unique PPT generation requirements without disrupting the existing system. This proactive step ensures scalability and modularity for onboarding new clients.

Finally, to prevent accidental client notifications during the intensive development and testing of these new features, email and WhatsApp notification triggers were temporarily commented out. Commit `51059b78f45d` specifically highlights this safety measure, ensuring that development work could proceed without the risk of sending premature or incorrect information to clients, thereby maintaining a controlled testing environment.

## Tradeoffs

While the solutions implemented this week significantly enhance our PPT generation capabilities and data completeness, they also introduce several inherent tradeoffs that warrant consideration.

Firstly, the reliance on external APIs and Large Language Models (LLMs) for critical functions like market creation and naming introduces potential points of failure and dependencies beyond our direct control. Services like Valhalla isochrone, `getvariables-internal-data-in`, and OpenAI's GPT-4o-mini are subject to external network latency, service availability, and rate limits. This means that the performance and reliability of our dynamic market creation system are directly tied to the performance and uptime of these third-party services. Furthermore, there are cost implications associated with API calls, particularly for LLMs, which need to be carefully monitored and managed to ensure cost-effectiveness at scale. Any changes in API contracts or pricing models from these external providers could necessitate significant adjustments on our end.

Secondly, the iterative debugging process for the Lenskart (LK) custom market creation, spanning from "v0 to v23" across numerous commits, clearly indicates a significant development complexity and a substantial time investment for a single feature. While this iterative approach ultimately led to a robust solution, it highlights the inherent challenges in integrating multiple geospatial services, external data sources, and AI models. This level of complexity implies that future enhancements or modifications to this system will likely require similar levels of dedicated engineering effort, potentially impacting the development velocity for other features. The extensive debugging also points to the difficulty in anticipating all edge cases and interactions when dealing with diverse external systems and geospatial data.

Thirdly, a specific debugging challenge encountered was the distinction between `json.dumps(data)` and `json=data` when making `requests.post` calls. While `requests` typically handles `json=data` by automatically serializing the dictionary to JSON, some APIs, as we discovered, are stricter and require the payload to be explicitly pre-serialized using `json.dumps(data)` and passed via the `data` parameter. This seemingly minor technical nuance required specific debugging time to identify and resolve, highlighting potential inconsistencies in API client expectations or strict payload requirements from the target endpoints. Such subtle differences can consume valuable engineering hours and underscore the importance of thorough API documentation and testing.

Fourth, commenting out client notifications (email and WhatsApp) during the testing phase is a practical and necessary safety measure to prevent unintended alerts. However, this approach requires careful management to ensure that these critical notification triggers are correctly re-enabled for production deployments. There's a risk, albeit mitigated by robust deployment checklists and code reviews, that these lines of code could inadvertently remain commented out, leading to a breakdown in client communication for successful PPT generations. This tradeoff necessitates strict adherence to deployment protocols and automated checks to prevent such oversights.

Finally, the extensive geospatial computations and data retrieval involved in defining and enriching new markets can be resource-intensive. Calculating drive-time isochrones, performing spatial queries against large datasets, and fetching demographic/POI variables for each custom market requires significant processing power and network bandwidth. While these operations are critical for data completeness, they could potentially impact the performance and response times for on-demand market creation, especially if multiple requests are processed concurrently. Optimizations and caching strategies may be required in the future to mitigate these performance implications as the system scales.

## Notable Tech

This week's engineering efforts leveraged a powerful stack of technologies, each playing a crucial role in enabling the advanced automation and geospatial intelligence features.

*   **Python `requests` library**: This fundamental library was extensively used for making HTTP requests to various internal and external APIs. It facilitated communication with the `retailapp/stg/v3/store/generateReport` endpoint for automated report generation, the Valhalla isochrone service for drive-time polygon calculations, and the `getvariables-internal-data-in` API for demographic and POI data retrieval. Its flexibility in handling different payload types (e.g., `json.dumps(data)` for specific API requirements) was key to successful integration.

*   **GeoPandas and Shapely**: These Python libraries formed the backbone of our geospatial data manipulation and analysis. GeoPandas, which extends the data types of pandas to allow spatial operations on geometric types, was critical for loading and processing geographical dataframes from our databases. Shapely, a library for planar geometry manipulation, provided the underlying geometric objects and operations (e.g., creating polygons, checking for intersections) necessary for determining if a property fell within an existing market, defining new market boundaries, and performing complex spatial queries.

*   **PostgreSQL**: As our primary relational database, PostgreSQL, particularly with its PostGIS extension, was central to storing and querying all geospatial data. We interacted with multiple PostgreSQL instances (`geoiqdb`, `geoiqdataserving`, `geoiq_retail_app_v3`) to retrieve existing market polygons, store newly generated custom markets, and manage property data. The ability of PostgreSQL to handle spatial data types and perform efficient spatial queries was indispensable.

*   **OpenAI GPT-4o-mini**: This advanced Large Language Model (LLM) was integrated to provide AI-driven market name generation. By feeding it contextual information, such as nearby Points of Interest (POIs) and addresses, GPT-4o-mini intelligently derived intuitive and human-readable names for the dynamically created markets. This innovative use of AI streamlined the naming process and ensured consistency where manual naming would be impractical.

*   **Google Cloud Functions (Valhalla isochrone, getvariables-internal-data-in)**: Google Cloud Functions were utilized as serverless compute environments to host and execute specialized geospatial analytics services. Specifically, one function wrapped the Valhalla isochrone service, allowing us to calculate precise drive-time polygons around properties. Another function provided access to the `getvariables-internal-data-in` endpoint, enabling the retrieval of rich demographic and POI variables for market definitions. Using Cloud Functions provided scalability, managed infrastructure, and secure access to these critical services.

*   **Google Cloud Storage (GCS)**: GCS served as a reliable and scalable object storage solution for accessing various utility and configuration data. This included files like `BrandDetails.csv`, `LK_cities_1630.csv`, `lenskart_stores.csv`, and `City-Hub Summary Updated.xlsx`. These files provided essential reference data, brand-specific configurations, and existing store locations, which were crucial inputs for the market creation logic and other PPT generation processes.
